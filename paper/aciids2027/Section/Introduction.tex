\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Visual Question Answering (VQA) asks a model to answer a natural-language question about an
image. Large vision--language models (VLMs) such as BLIP-2~\cite{li2023blip2},
Flamingo~\cite{alayrac2022flamingo} and LLaVA~\cite{liu2023visualinstructiontuning} have advanced
the task rapidly. They connect a pre-trained vision encoder to a pre-trained language model and
generate free-form answers. Vietnamese has followed this trend. The open model
Vintern-1B~\cite{doan2024vintern1b} connects an InternViT-300M encoder~\cite{chen2024internvl} to
a Qwen2.5-0.5B decoder~\cite{qwen2025qwen25}. Benchmarks such as ViVQA~\cite{tran2021vivqa} and
the reasoning-oriented AutoViVQA~\cite{autovivqa2026} measure progress in this low-resource
setting.

However, building a strong model for a new Vietnamese benchmark is still expensive. The common
recipe fine-tunes an existing VLM. The fine-tuned Vintern-1B baseline of AutoViVQA adds LoRA
adapters to the language model and splits each image into up to six high-resolution tiles plus a
thumbnail. Its decoder thus reads up to 1{,}792 visual tokens per image. Another option is a
task-specific architecture, such as ViMoE-VQA~\cite{vimoevqa} with its sparse mixture-of-experts
fusion module. A cheaper option keeps the whole VLM frozen and trains only a small connector.
Existing connectors follow two designs. A \emph{global-only bridge} maps the global image vector
to a few tokens. It is cheap, but it loses local details such as small objects, printed text and
spatial relations. A \emph{learned patch compressor}, such as a Q-Former, keeps these details.
However, it must learn a new mapping from visual features to the decoder, which is difficult with
limited Vietnamese data.

Vintern-1B already offers a third option. Its projector was pre-trained to map image regions into
the input space of the decoder, so this alignment does not need to be learned again. Meanwhile,
the model discards the global \texttt{[CLS]} vector of its encoder, which summarises the whole
scene. We therefore study two questions. With limited labelled data, is reusing the pre-trained
alignment of a VLM more effective than learning a new connector? And how many global and local
tokens does the frozen decoder need?

To this end, we propose \textbf{ViBridge-VQA}. It gives the frozen decoder of Vintern-1B two
complementary kinds of visual tokens from a single image view. \emph{Local} tokens reuse the
frozen projector, average-pooled to a chosen budget and placed in the original image slot. The
decoder thus receives region features in the representation it was trained to read.
\emph{Global} tokens are produced from the \texttt{[CLS]} vector by a small trainable bridge, the
only trained part. They add the scene-level information that Vintern-1B ignores. Since every
additional token increases inference cost, we treat the number of tokens as a budget. We select
it as the knee of the accuracy--cost Pareto front. ViBridge-VQA performs on par with the
fine-tuned Vintern-1B without updating any of its weights, while its decoder reads far fewer
visual tokens.

In summary, our main contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item We propose \textbf{ViBridge-VQA}, a global--local bridge for a frozen Vietnamese VLM. It
  combines trainable global tokens with local tokens from the VLM's own pre-trained projector,
  and it can be combined with a lightweight decoder LoRA.
  \item We treat the number of visual tokens as an explicit \textbf{accuracy--cost budget},
  selected on validation as the knee of the Pareto front.
  \item We \textbf{analyse where the gains come from}. Local tokens matter more than global ones,
  and reusing the projector beats learning a new connector. Local tokens also complement decoder
  adaptation and transfer to other Vietnamese VQA datasets.
\end{itemize}
