\section{Ablation: Hunting the Bottleneck}
\label{sec:ablation}

The recipe leaves an F1 gap to ViMoE-VQA. This section works through
\textbf{six candidate axes} for closing it --- each a different place to spend a
small extra budget --- and asks which one actually moves the needle. Anchor
throughout: \multitoken{}-plain, 4-seed 2-epoch, F1 $49.55$ / CIDEr-D $92.30$.

\subsection{Bridge architecture and capacity (RQ1--RQ2)}
\label{sec:abl-bridge}

Table~\ref{tab:abl-bridges} lists the five bridges on the frozen backbone.
\textbf{RQ1 --- is a simple bridge enough?} Yes: \multitoken{} ($0.78\%$) is the
best bridge and already beats Vintern-1B fine-tuned on generation metrics.
\textbf{RQ2 --- does a bigger bridge help?} No: the Full Q-Former has
$10\times$ the parameters and scores \emph{lower} (F1 $47.4$ vs.\ $49.6$), and
\multitoken{} has the lowest validation cross-entropy of the five. Bridge
capacity is not the binding constraint.

\subsection{Visual tiles (RQ3)}
\label{sec:abl-tiles}

\textbf{RQ3 --- does spending the tile lever help?} No --- it breaks the bridge.
Feeding the 1-tile-trained \multitoken{} more tiles at inference collapses it
(Table~\ref{tab:abl-tiles}, Fig.~\ref{fig:tile-collapse}): F1 $50.7 \to 21$, val
loss $1.48 \to 3.36$. Mean-pooling 8 output tokens over 3--6$\times$ as many
patches washes out the signal. We also retrained the oracle bridges \emph{with}
tile-count augmentation and re-ran the analysis of Sec.~\ref{sec:abl-routing} on
those checkpoints: \multitoken{} still collapses beyond one tile even when it saw
multiple tiles in training (CIDEr $0.90 \to 0.47$ at 3 tiles); the
cross-attention bridges stay flat. \textbf{One tile is the operating point this
architecture is built for, not a compromise} --- spending more of the lever is
strictly worse.

\subsection{Adaptive routing (RQ4)}
\label{sec:abl-routing}

\textbf{RQ4 --- could a policy spend the tile lever adaptively, per sample,
informed by question type (ViMoE's ``reasoning-aware'' idea) or cheap visual
state?} No.

\paragraph{Oracle.}
Action space $A = \text{bridge} \times \ntiles$, bridge $\in$ \{\multitoken,
qformer, mini\_qformer\}, $\ntiles \in \{1,3,6\}$, $|A| = 9$; cost
$C(a) = \ntiles/6$, quality $M(a;x) = $ per-sample CIDEr of the greedy answer.
Bridges are the tile-augmented retrains (closes the ``never saw $>1$ tile''
confound). On the $|A|=9$ held-out test set ($3{,}739$ samples), the oracle
$a^\ast(x,0)$ scores mean CIDEr $1.259$ (cost $0.361$) vs.\ the best fixed action
\bridge{multi\_token$|$t1} at $0.902$ (cost $0.167$) --- a $+0.36$ CIDEr
($+40\%$) headroom, spread evenly across all 8 reasoning categories
(per-category headroom $+0.28$ to $+0.65$), \emph{not} concentrated in a subset
of reasoning types.

\paragraph{The headroom is memorizable noise, not a learnable signal.}
A policy MLP trained \emph{and} evaluated on the same test split reproduces the
oracle ($a^\ast$-match $0.98$, mean CIDEr $1.26$). But $a^\ast$ does not
transfer: the val and test oracle-$a^\ast$ distributions disagree (majority
\bridge{qformer$|$t1} on val, \bridge{multi\_token$|$t1} on test) despite
identical stratification, because per-sample CIDEr on single 4-word answers is a
poor estimator of relative quality among near-tied actions.

\paragraph{Policy arms.}
Three arms of $\pi_\theta((\cdot,\lambda) \to a)$ trained against $a^\ast$:
\bridge{ours} $= P(r{\mid}Q) + f(I,Q)$; \bridge{rt\_only} $= P(r{\mid}Q)$;
\bridge{visual\_only} $= f(I,Q)$. On held-out test (Table~\ref{tab:abl-policy})
\textbf{all three converge onto} \bridge{fixed: multi\_token$|$t1},
$\lambda$-independent, $a^\ast$-match $\approx$ the majority-class rate
($0.43$--$0.46$). Adding the reasoning-type posterior changes nothing. No learned
routing policy beats the fixed best-bridge / minimum-tiles policy --- and
reasoning-type labels, which on this auto-generated dataset largely encode
question surface form (router F1 $0.91$), contribute nothing on top.

\subsection{Training signal and representation alignment (RQ5)}
\label{sec:abl-rq5}

\textbf{RQ5 --- does a better training target or a better vision--language
alignment close the gap?} No (Table~\ref{tab:abl-rq5}, all 3-seed, anchor F1
$49.55$). \texttt{answer-sampling=random} slightly hurts. \texttt{align-feat} ---
distilling the bridge toward Vintern's \emph{own} pre-aligned connector --- is an
\textbf{absolute null} at 3 seeds (\dF{}~$-0.03$): the bridge is already at the
alignment the frozen decoder can use. \texttt{align-logit} at $\alpha=1.0$ is a
known mis-weighting (the KL term swamps the generation objective); since the
correctly-weighted \texttt{feat} variant already shows zero benefit, we did not
pursue a weight sweep. \textbf{Nothing on the training or alignment axis moves
F1.}

\subsection{Decoder capacity: LoRA (RQ6)}
\label{sec:abl-rq6}

\textbf{RQ6 --- does opening the decoder itself help?} \textbf{Yes --- this is the
one axis that moves F1}, and it is the only intervention that touches the
decoder.

\paragraph{Per bridge.}
Table~\ref{tab:abl-lora-bridge} gives the LoRA lift for each bridge. The five
plain bridges span F1 $45.2$--$49.6$ / CIDEr-D $79$--$92$ --- real quality
differences across three token-mixing designs. After a $0.23\%$ LoRA on the
decoder, \textbf{all five converge to F1 $52.6$--$53.2$ / CIDEr-D
$100.8$--$103.0$} (Fig.~\ref{fig:bridge-equalizing}); the lift is larger for
weaker bridges ($+3.6 \to +7.8$). Once the decoder has capacity to use whatever
representation it is given, \emph{which bridge supplies it stops mattering}. A
paired bootstrap over the $5{,}463$ val samples (seed-42, 2-epoch) confirms every
plain$\to$LoRA lift: \dF{} $95\%$ CIs $[2.94, 4.14]$ (\multitoken{}) to
$[7.80, 9.16]$ (\bridge{tile\_attention}), $\Delta$CIDEr-D $[7.15, 10.99]$ to
$[21.86, 26.54]$, $P(\Delta{>}0) = 1.000$ on every one; the monotonic widening
is the equalization pattern in the per-sample deltas.

\paragraph{Where in the decoder.}
Table~\ref{tab:abl-lora-target}: the usable decoder headroom is
\textbf{specifically in attention}. LoRA on the feed-forward blocks diverges
training (val loss $2$--$4$ vs.\ $1.37$). \emph{Caveat:} this may be a
hyperparameter artifact ($\alpha=32$ is likely too strong for the wider MLP
dimension); we limit the claim to the recipe's configuration.

\paragraph{Rank.}
A 5-point rank sweep ($r \in \{4,8,16,32,64\}$, 600-sample subset) initially
looked monotonic on one seed; with 3-seed means, $r{=}32$ ($53.83 \pm 1.77$) and
$r{=}64$ ($54.06 \pm 0.94$) are statistically indistinguishable. We keep
\textbf{r=16} --- the value with full-val 3-seed rigour --- and make no
``higher rank is better'' claim.

\paragraph{Training duration.}
1 epoch $\to$ 3 epochs adds F1 $53.17 \to 54.67$ ($+1.5$), CIDEr-D
$101.7 \to 106.8$, closing the F1 gap to ViMoE from $7.5$ to $6.0$; most of the
gain lands by epoch 2. The 1-epoch numbers are the headline; the 3-epoch result
is the strongest form.

\subsection{Summary: six axes, one positive}
\label{sec:abl-summary}

Table~\ref{tab:abl-summary} lays out all six axes. Four independent axes on the
vision / training side produce no lift; \texttt{feat}-alignment is an
\emph{absolute} null. The one axis that touches the decoder produces a clear lift
on every bridge, and localizes further to the decoder's \textbf{attention}. This
\emph{pattern across six interventions} --- not any single ablation --- is what
localizes the bottleneck: for this VLM class (frozen ViT, frozen 0.5B decoder, a
few pooled vision tokens), the frozen decoder's attention is the ceiling, not the
vision pipeline. Opening it by $0.23\%$ recovers a third or more of the F1 gap to
a fully-unfrozen prior-work baseline. Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion} develops this
reading; the paper's primary contribution remains the ${\sim}1\%$ recipe
(Sec.~\ref{sec:results}) --- the diagnosis explains \emph{why} it tops out where
it does rather than arguing it should be abandoned.
