\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

\subsection{Dataset and Evaluation Metrics}

\paragraph{Dataset.}
We evaluate on AutoViVQA~\cite{autovivqa2026}, a Vietnamese VQA benchmark built from
MS COCO images~\cite{lin2015coco} with an LLM-driven generation pipeline and
ensemble-based quality control. Each question has five short free-form answers
(1--10 words) and is labelled with one of nine question types. The public
release is a random 80/20 train/validation split (29{,}661 / 7{,}416 questions),
in which questions about the same image can fall into different splits. We
remove 273 duplicated (image, question) pairs and 97 questions with irregular
type labels, 77 of which belong to the rare \emph{comparison} type, and keep eight
reasoning categories. We then re-partition the remaining
36{,}707 questions by image into a grouped 70/15/15 split (seed 42), so that no
image appears in more than one split (Table~\ref{tab:data}). The category
distribution is kept approximately equal across splits. On our bridges, the
grouped split lowers F1 by at most 0.5 points compared with a random split, so
image leakage has a small effect, but we use the grouped split for every number
reported here.

\paragraph{Evaluation metrics.}
Following AutoViVQA and ViMoE-VQA, we report answer-matching metrics (exact-match
Accuracy, token Precision, Recall and F1) and generation metrics
(BLEU~\cite{papineni2002bleu}, ROUGE-L~\cite{lin2004rouge},
METEOR~\cite{banerjee2005meteor}, CIDEr~\cite{vedantam2015cider}), all scaled by
100. Token-level scores are computed against each of the five references and the
best match is kept. Our F1 is the mean of per-sample F1 scores, which is never
larger than the harmonic mean $2PR/(P+R)$ of the averaged precision and recall.
Most published baseline F1 values match the latter convention; under it, our best
configuration would score 55.50 instead of 54.28 on test, so our F1 values are
conservative.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{Model configuration.}
InternViT-300M and Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct are initialised from
Vintern-1B-v3.5~\cite{doan2024vintern1b} and kept frozen. The bridge is trained for
2 epochs, after which validation cross-entropy and CIDEr no longer improve; the
LoRA stage runs for 1 or 3 epochs. We use AdamW with a learning rate of
$2\times10^{-4}$ and an effective batch size of 8. Training uses the first of the
five reference answers and evaluation uses all five. Answers are generated with
greedy decoding. A bridge run takes about 5 hours on a single Tesla P100 (16\,GB).
Each configuration is trained with 3 seeds (42, 123, 3407); the Multi-Token bridge
additionally uses seed 2026. The test split is only used for final reporting.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
We compare against three groups: (1) multimodal models fine-tuned on AutoViVQA
(ViT5\_ViT, BARTPhoBEiT~\cite{tran2023bartphobeitpretrainedsequencetosequenceimage}
and Vintern-1B~\cite{doan2024vintern1b}); (2) models used zero-shot (Vintern-1B,
LLaMA~3.2, Gemini~2.0 and 2.5 Flash, GPT-5); and (3)
ViMoE-VQA~\cite{vimoevqa}. Results of groups (1) and (2) are taken from the
AutoViVQA benchmark~\cite{autovivqa2026} and the results of ViMoE-VQA from its
paper; both were obtained on an 8:1:1 split of the original release and are
therefore not strictly comparable with our grouped split. The fine-tuned
Vintern-1B follows the official fine-tuning recipe of
Vintern~\cite{doan2024vintern1b}: the vision encoder and the projector are
frozen, a rank-16 LoRA is applied to the language model, each image is encoded
as up to six $448\times448$ tiles plus a thumbnail, and training runs for one
epoch. It therefore trains a comparable fraction of parameters (about 0.2\% versus
1.01\% for ViBridge-VQA), but encodes up to seven views per image and passes 256
visual tokens per view to the decoder, whereas ViBridge-VQA encodes one view and
passes 8 tokens.

Table~\ref{tab:main} compares ViBridge-VQA with the baselines. With only the bridge
trained (0.78\% of the parameters), the model already exceeds the fine-tuned
Vintern-1B on BLEU, METEOR and CIDEr. Adding the decoder LoRA for 3 epochs brings
F1 to 54.28, on par with the fine-tuned Vintern-1B (53.76), and yields the best
BLEU, ROUGE-L, METEOR and CIDEr among all compared systems, with gains of
$+14.6$ BLEU, $+9.6$ METEOR and $+34.8$ CIDEr over the fine-tuned Vintern-1B. The
fine-tuned Vintern-1B keeps the highest exact-match accuracy, and ViMoE-VQA keeps
the highest precision and F1 ($+6.4$ over ViBridge-VQA), so a frozen-backbone
adaptation does not yet close the gap in answer-token overlap to a model trained
for the task.

Compared with zero-shot large language models, ViBridge-VQA is better on every
metric except recall. As already observed for AutoViVQA and ViMoE-VQA, zero-shot
models produce long answers that cover many reference words (high recall) at the
cost of precision. ViBridge-VQA, trained on the short answer style of the
benchmark, achieves a balanced precision--recall trade-off. One epoch of LoRA
already provides 77\% of the 3-epoch F1 gain.

\paragraph{Statistical reliability.}
ViBridge-VQA is stable across random seeds (Table~\ref{tab:stability}): the
standard deviation of F1 is at most 0.18 and that of CIDEr at most 0.71. Test F1
is 0.35--0.43 points below validation F1 for all three configurations, which shows
that no configuration was over-fitted to the validation split. A paired bootstrap
over the 5{,}463 validation questions (2{,}000 resamples, seed 42) gives a 95\%
confidence interval of $[3.49, 4.65]$ for the F1 gain of the 1-epoch LoRA over the
bridge.

\subsection{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:ablation}

All ablations are measured on the validation split and compared with the
Multi-Token bridge without LoRA (F1 49.55).

\paragraph{Bridge architecture and decoder adaptation.}
Table~\ref{tab:bridges} and Fig.~\ref{fig:results}a compare the five bridges. Without
LoRA, they span 45.17--49.55 F1. The Multi-Token bridge is the best and has the
lowest validation cross-entropy, while the Full Q-Former, with $9.4\times$ more
parameters, is 2.20 F1 lower: a larger bridge does not help a frozen decoder.
Decoder LoRA improves \emph{every} bridge, and the improvement is larger for weaker
bridges ($+7.8$ for Tile-Attention, $+4.0$ for Multi-Token). After LoRA, the five
bridges lie within 0.9 F1 (52.64--53.52) and 2.5 CIDEr points, compared with 4.4
F1 and 12.3 CIDEr points before. Paired bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals of the
gain (seed 42) lie above zero for all five bridges. Once the decoder can adapt, the choice of
bridge therefore matters little. On a 600-question validation subset, ranks 32 and 64
do not improve over rank 16 beyond seed variation.

\paragraph{Training signal and representation alignment.}
Table~\ref{tab:levers} tests whether the bridge can be improved without touching
the decoder. Sampling one of the five reference answers at random in each epoch,
instead of always the first, lowers F1 by 1.47. Distilling the bridge towards the
pre-aligned projector of Vintern-1B has no effect at the feature level ($-0.03$)
or at the logit level with a small weight ($+0.20$, $\lambda=0.1$), and degrades the
model when the distillation term dominates ($-8.80$, $\lambda=1$). The bridge is
therefore already as well aligned as the frozen decoder can exploit, and the only
intervention with a clear gain is decoder adaptation.

\subsection{Analysis by Reasoning Type}

Fig.~\ref{fig:results}b breaks down the effect of decoder adaptation by the
reasoning categories of AutoViVQA. The gain grows with the length of the expected
answer: it is largest for causal ($+7.4$) and relational ($+4.8$) questions, whose
reference answers are the longest (5.0 and 5.6 words on average), and vanishes for
counting ($+0.1$), whose answers are the shortest (2.8 words). For counting, the
adapted decoder corrects 37 answers but breaks 29 others (F1 threshold 0.5), so the
gains cancel out; with the bridge alone already at 66.6 F1, the remaining counting
errors concern the visual count itself rather than the phrasing of the answer. By
contrast, it corrects 140 causal answers while breaking 58. We also
note that 3.7\% of all AutoViVQA questions, mostly counting questions, refer to the
caption used to generate them (e.g.\ ``how many groups of people are mentioned in
the description?'') rather than to the image; decoder adaptation does not change
the score on this subset either ($+0.3$).

\subsection{Qualitative Analysis and Error Discussion}

\begin{figure}[t]

answers, Bridge: Multi-Token bridge without LoRA, +LoRA: ViBridge-VQA (3 epochs).
Green: correct, red: incorrect. Example~1 is answered correctly by both models; in

\label{fig:qual}
\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:qual} shows representative predictions of ViBridge-VQA. The adapted
model correctly answers simple spatial questions (Example~1, ``on the table''),
gives the expected intent for causal questions (Example~2, ``for fun''), counts
clearly separated objects (Example~3) and recognises attributes such as colour
(Example~4). In Examples~2--4 the bridge alone already receives the image but
produces a wrong or implausible answer (``to pose'', ``one sink'', ``blue''), which
illustrates that decoder adaptation mainly improves how the visual evidence is
turned into an answer.

Two error patterns remain. First, counting is not improved on average: corrections
such as Example~3 are offset by a similar number of new errors (37 versus 29).
Second, some attribute errors are near misses between visually similar values,
for example ``red'' for an orange bus. Both patterns are
consistent with the bridge reading a single global image vector, which limits
fine-grained visual detail, and suggest that further gains require a richer visual
input rather than a larger bridge.
