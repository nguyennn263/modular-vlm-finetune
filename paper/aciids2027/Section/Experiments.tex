\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

\subsection{Dataset and Metrics}
\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{Dataset.}
We use AutoViVQA~\cite{autovivqa2026}, built on MS COCO images~\cite{lin2015coco}, where each
question has five short free-form answers and a reasoning type. After removing 273 duplicated
(image, question) pairs and 97 questions without a reasoning label, we split the remaining
36{,}707 questions 70/15/15 by image (seed 42), so no test image is seen in training: 25{,}776 /
5{,}463 / 5{,}468 questions on 13{,}579 / 2{,}909 / 2{,}910 images. This split is stricter than a
question-level one, on which Global-only scores about 0.5 F1 higher.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
Following AutoViVQA and ViMoE-VQA, we report Accuracy (Acc), word-level Precision (Prec), Recall (Rec) and F1,
BLEU~\cite{papineni2002bleu}, ROUGE-L (R-L)~\cite{lin2004rouge}, METEOR (MET)~\cite{banerjee2005meteor} and
CIDEr~\cite{vedantam2015cider} against the five references. Precision, Recall and F1 compare the
word sets of the answer and of the reference with the highest F1, and are averaged over questions.
Accuracy is the share of answers identical to a reference, ignoring case; BLEU is corpus-level BLEU-4 on
whitespace tokens.

\subsection{Setup}

\paragraph{Implementation details.}
The encoder, projector and decoder come from Vintern-1B-v3.5~\cite{doan2024vintern1b} and stay
frozen. We train with AdamW (learning rate $2\times10^{-4}$, effective batch size 8) on the first
reference answer. Bridges are trained for two epochs, after which the validation loss and CIDEr
stop improving. Models with a decoder LoRA are trained for one epoch, with the bridge (if any) and
the LoRA learned jointly from scratch. Global-only + LoRA is also reported after three epochs, its
best setting, which Table~\ref{tab:ood} and Fig.~\ref{fig:types} use. Decoding is greedy with at
most 50 new tokens, and every model fits on one 16\,GB NVIDIA T4. Each configuration is trained
with three seeds (42, 123, 3407), four for Global-only (adding 2026), and tables report mean
$\pm$ std.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
Table~\ref{tab:main} compares with two groups of models: (1) fine-tuned on AutoViVQA:
ViT5\_ViT, BARTPhoBEiT~\cite{tran2023bartphobeitpretrainedsequencetosequenceimage}, Vintern-1B and
ViMoE-VQA~\cite{vimoevqa}, and (2) zero-shot: Vintern-1B, LLaMA~3.2, Gemini~2.0 and 2.5
Flash, and GPT-5. The fine-tuned
Vintern-1B follows the official recipe: six $448\times448$ tiles plus a thumbnail per test image
and a rank-16 LoRA on the language model for one epoch. The analysis also uses
\emph{Global-only}, the bridge of Eq.~\eqref{eq:bridge} with $g=8$ and no local tokens, with and
without a decoder LoRA. Our models are \emph{ViBridge-VQA} ($g=14$, $k=36$) and
\emph{ViBridge-VQA + LoRA}.

\paragraph{ViBridge-VQA nears fine-tuned performance.}
Without updating any weight of Vintern-1B, ViBridge-VQA raises its F1 from 17.55 (zero-shot) to
54.56 (Table~\ref{tab:main}). This is comparable to the reported 53.76 of the fine-tuned Vintern-1B, on a stricter split, while the decoder
receives 50 instead of 1{,}792 visual tokens and only 1.35\% of the parameters are trained. On test, the bridge alone
with the same 14 global tokens reaches 51.02 F1 ($\pm$0.10), so the 36 local tokens add 3.54 F1.

\paragraph{LoRA adds 3.6 F1.}
With a decoder LoRA, ViBridge-VQA reaches 58.17 F1 (+3.61) with 1.57\% of the parameters trained,
and gives the best Accuracy (14.67), BLEU (22.53), ROUGE-L (56.25) and METEOR (49.32). Results
are stable across seeds (test std of F1 at most 0.19) and splits (test F1 within 0.4 of validation).

Table~\ref{tab:bridges} compares three sources of visual tokens. \emph{Residual} (one token: a linear projection plus a two-layer MLP
correction) and \emph{Global-only} (Eq.~\eqref{eq:bridge}, $g=8$) read the \texttt{[CLS]} vector. Five bridges
learn to compress the 577 encoder tokens: \emph{Tile-Attention} (self-attention and eight learned
queries), the light and full Q-Former~\cite{li2023blip2} (7 and 15 learned queries plus an anchor
token from the mean encoder feature; two and four layers; the full one also reads the question), C-Abstractor~\cite{cha2024honeybee} (convolutions around a
$3\times3$ pooling, 9 tokens) and
\emph{Patch-pool} (a linear projection averaged into eight bands). All are randomly initialised and
trained with the same recipe. The third
source is our local tokens.

\paragraph{Learned compressors fall short.}
The five learned compressors stay 1.9--5.6 F1 below Global-only, despite up to $9.4\times$ more
parameters. With limited data, a new mapping from patches to the decoder is hard to learn, and
more capacity does not help: the 69.4M-parameter full Q-Former is no better than the 27.6M light
one.

\paragraph{Reusing the pre-aligned projector is more data-efficient.}
Our results suggest that, under this setup, the same patch features help once they pass through
the pre-trained projector. Adding 36 or 144
local tokens to Global-only ($g=8$) raises F1 by 3.58 and 5.41 (Table~\ref{tab:budget}). At a
similar number of tokens, the projector wins with fewer trained parameters: 17 tokens ($g=8$,
$k=9$) give 52.82 F1 against 46.84 for the 16-token full Q-Former, and 9 tokens ($g=8$, $k=1$) give
51.03 against 48.86 for the 9-token C-Abstractor. Pooling alone is not the reason: Patch-pool
also averages, but after a new projection, and reaches only 45.78 F1.
Distilling the projector into Global-only does not help either: 50.35 F1 with a cosine loss on the
mean tokens and 50.46 with a KL loss on the answers, against 50.74 without distillation.

\paragraph{Local tokens matter more than global tokens.}
Table~\ref{tab:budget} and Fig.~\ref{fig:results}b vary $g\in\{8,14\}$ and $k\in\{1,9,36,144\}$.
At $g=14$, going from 1 to 144 local tokens adds 4.94 F1 and 19.05 CIDEr, whereas going from 8 to
14 global tokens adds only 0.2--0.8 F1 at a fixed $k$. The gain per token falls from 0.07 F1 (23 to
50 tokens) to 0.015 F1 (50 to 158 tokens).

\paragraph{The knee is $g=14$, $k=36$.}
The knee of the Pareto front (Section~\ref{sec:budget}, Fig.~\ref{fig:results}b) is
$g=14$, $k=36$ for each of the eight validation metrics, and also with generation time as the
cost. TOPSIS with equal weights also ranks it first. $k=144$ is 1.65 F1 higher, but $k=36$ obtains 72\% of its gain over
Global-only (67\% over the 14-token bridge of Table~\ref{tab:gsweep}) with 32\% of its tokens.

\paragraph{Global tokens peak at $g=14$.}
Table~\ref{tab:gsweep} and Fig.~\ref{fig:results}a vary $g$ from 4 to 18 for Global-only.
Validation F1 rises from 48.98 ($g=4$) to 50.74 ($g=8$) and 51.56 ($g=14$), then falls to 51.20

\paragraph{Fewer visual tokens, one encoder pass.}
Compared with the official tiling, ViBridge-VQA reduces the visual tokens at the decoder input
from 1{,}792 to 50, and the encoder computation from seven $448\times448$ views (362 GMACs each)
to one $336\times336$ view (191 GMACs), i.e., 13 times fewer encoder GMACs. Within our budgets,
$k=144$ makes generation about 15\% slower than $k=36$.

\paragraph{LoRA gains more with local tokens.}
ViBridge-VQA + LoRA is the best model on every metric of Table~\ref{tab:lora} (58.17 F1, 122.88
CIDEr), and LoRA gains more on ViBridge-VQA than on Global-only for all eight metrics (+3.61 versus

frozen decoder, a trained bridge is needed: local tokens alone give 27.55 F1, mostly because the
decoder then answers in long sentences (24.8 words on average).

\label{sec:analysis}

\paragraph{Local tokens help most on visible content.}
In Fig.~\ref{fig:types}, ViBridge-VQA adds 11.0 F1 over
Global-only on recognition, 6.4 on action and 5.8 on spatial questions, and beats Global-only +
LoRA on these types without adapting the decoder. Decoder adaptation helps more on causal and
relational questions, which have the longest reference answers (5.0 and 5.6 words), and on the 66 yes/no
questions. ViBridge-VQA + LoRA is the best of the four models on seven of the eight types.

\paragraph{More exact answers, fewer misses.}
On validation, answers are as long as the references (4.3 words on average), and most of them
(77--81\%) partially overlap the closest reference. Compared with Global-only, ViBridge-VQA raises
exact matches (F1 = 1) from 9.7\% to 13.3\% and lowers answers with no word in common with any
reference (F1 = 0) from 9.6\% to 8.0\%; with LoRA, these reach 15.7\% and
6.8\%.

\paragraph{LoRA makes answers precise.}
In Fig.~\ref{fig:qual}, ViBridge-VQA already finds the relevant content, such as the driver's
phone or the helmets, but its answers are vague. The decoder LoRA makes them precise: the driver is
using \emph{the phone}, and the helmets are worn \emph{to protect their heads}.
