\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}



\subsection{Dataset and Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{Dataset.}
We evaluate on AutoViVQA~\cite{autovivqa2026}, a Vietnamese VQA benchmark on MS COCO
images~\cite{lin2015coco} in which each question has five short free-form reference answers and
a reasoning type. To measure answers about images the model has never seen, we use an
image-disjoint split. After removing 273 duplicated (image, question) pairs and 97 questions
without one of the eight reasoning labels, we partition the 36{,}707 questions by image into a
70/15/15 split (seed 42), so that no image appears in more than one split: 25{,}776 / 5{,}463 / 5{,}468
questions on 13{,}579 / 2{,}909 / 2{,}910 images. In a preliminary comparison, this split lowered
the F1 of the global-only bridge by about 0.5 points relative to a split that does not group
questions by image.

\paragraph{Evaluation metrics.}
Following AutoViVQA and ViMoE-VQA, we report exact-match Accuracy, word-level Precision, Recall
and F1, BLEU~\cite{papineni2002bleu}, ROUGE-L~\cite{lin2004rouge},
METEOR~\cite{banerjee2005meteor} and CIDEr~\cite{vedantam2015cider}, all against the five
reference answers and multiplied by 100. All our models are scored with the same implementation.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{Implementation details.}
The vision encoder, the projector and the decoder are initialised from
Vintern-1B-v3.5~\cite{doan2024vintern1b} and kept frozen. ViBridge-VQA is trained for two
epochs, after which neither the validation loss nor CIDEr improves further; the variants with a decoder LoRA are trained for one epoch, and the global-only model with LoRA
also for three epochs, its strongest setting (+1.3 validation F1). We use AdamW with a learning rate
of $2\times10^{-4}$ and an effective batch size of 8, train on the first reference answer, and decode greedily (batch size 2, at most 50 new
tokens). Unless stated otherwise, every configuration is trained with three random
seeds (42, 123 and 3407), and we report the mean and the standard deviation. Every model fits on a single 16\,GB GPU such as an NVIDIA T4. The token budget is selected on the validation split, and the test
split is used only for the final report.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
We compare against three groups of systems: (1) multimodal models fine-tuned on
AutoViVQA, namely ViT5\_ViT,
BARTPhoBEiT~\cite{tran2023bartphobeitpretrainedsequencetosequenceimage}, Vintern-1B and
ViMoE-VQA~\cite{vimoevqa}; (2) models used zero-shot, namely Vintern-1B, LLaMA~3.2,
Gemini~2.0 and 2.5 Flash, and GPT-5; and (3) our \emph{global-only} model, which uses the
same bridge without local tokens ($k=0$, $g=8$), with and without a decoder LoRA. The results of groups (1) and (2) are those reported in the AutoViVQA and ViMoE-VQA papers. The fine-tuned Vintern-1B follows the
official recipe: the vision encoder and the projector are frozen, a rank-16 LoRA adapts the
language model for one epoch, and each image is encoded as up to six $448\times448$ tiles
plus a thumbnail.

Table~\ref{tab:main} compares ViBridge-VQA with the baselines. Without updating any
weight of Vintern-1B, ViBridge-VQA raises its F1 from 17.55 in the zero-shot setting to 54.56. With the selected budget of 50 visual tokens, its precision, recall and F1 are comparable to the published values of the fine-tuned
Vintern-1B, while its
exact-match accuracy is 1.3 points lower; with all 144 local tokens, its accuracy is also comparable (13.19 versus 13.01). Its decoder reads 50 or 158 visual tokens, 36 and 11 times fewer than the 1{,}792 of the
fine-tuned Vintern-1B.

Decoder adaptation adds to these gains rather
than replacing them. Training ViBridge-VQA
jointly with a rank-16 LoRA for a single epoch raises test F1 by a further 3.6 points to
58.17 and gives the highest exact-match accuracy in Table~\ref{tab:main}. The task-specific ViMoE-VQA, whose fusion module and decoder are trained on AutoViVQA, keeps the
highest precision and F1, 2.5 F1 points above our best model. ViBridge-VQA reaches its results
differently: instead of designing and training a new architecture, it reuses an existing VLM
and trains 1.57\% of its parameters, and it leads in exact-match accuracy. 

Compared with our global-only model (Table~\ref{tab:bridges}, Fig.~\ref{fig:results}a),
ViBridge-VQA with a frozen decoder already reaches the global-only model trained with a
three-epoch decoder LoRA (54.96 versus 54.78 validation F1), and with the same one-epoch LoRA it
is 4.7 F1 above it (58.21 versus 53.47). 

\paragraph{Statistical stability.}
On the test split, the standard deviation of ViBridge-VQA is at most 0.19 for F1 and 0.95
for CIDEr, and for both models in Table~\ref{tab:main}, test F1 lies at most 0.6 points
below validation F1.

Table~\ref{tab:budget} and Fig.~\ref{fig:results}a vary the number of global tokens
$g\in\{8,14\}$ and of local tokens $k\in\{1,9,36,144\}$. The most striking pattern is that \emph{adding local tokens improves F1 far more than adding
global tokens}. At $g=14$, increasing $k$ from 1 to
144 adds 4.9 F1 and 19.1 CIDEr on the validation split, whereas increasing $g$ from 8 to 14
at a fixed $k$ adds only 0.2--0.6 F1. Accuracy has not saturated within the 144 region
tokens available at our resolution, as each step of $k$ still adds 1.3--2.0 F1, but the gain
per token drops quickly, from 0.07 F1 per token between 23 and 50 tokens to 0.015 between
50 and 158. The budget is therefore a genuine trade-off between accuracy and cost.

Because accuracy grows with every added token, all configurations lie on the Pareto front,
and we select its knee (Section~\ref{sec:budget}). On the validation split, the knee is $g=14$, $k=36$, and it is the same for each of the eight
metrics, whether cost is counted in visual tokens or as evaluation runtime. As a post-hoc check that played no role
in the selection, the knee computed on the test split falls on the same configuration. TOPSIS on validation, with equal weight on accuracy and cost, makes the same choice.
It obtains 72\% of the F1 gain of $k=144$ over global only with 32\% of its tokens. It is a compromise between accuracy and cost, not the most accurate
configuration: $k=144$ is 1.7 F1 higher. $g=14$ is better than $g=8$ at every $k$.

\paragraph{Computational cost.}
We distinguish three kinds of cost. \emph{Decoder input}: with the official tiling (at most six tiles plus a thumbnail), every
AutoViVQA test image is split into six tiles plus a thumbnail, i.e., 1{,}792 visual tokens,
against 50 for ViBridge-VQA. \emph{Vision encoding}:
InternViT-300M processes 577 tokens for one $336\times336$ view, about 191 GMACs, against 1{,}025
tokens and 362 GMACs for each $448\times448$ view (analytic counts, matching fvcore at $448\times448$); with seven views, Vintern-1B thus needs 2{,}533 GMACs per image, 13 times more. \emph{Evaluation runtime}: within our own
budgets, keeping all 144 local tokens instead of 36 makes an evaluation session about 17\%
longer (Table~\ref{tab:budget}).

\paragraph{Where should the visual tokens come from?}
Table~\ref{tab:bridges} compares three sources of visual tokens: the global \texttt{[CLS]}
vector (a one-token \emph{Residual} bridge or the \emph{Multi-Token} bridge of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:bridge}), a learned compression of the patches (\emph{Tile-Attention} with eight
learned queries, or a light or full Q-Former~\cite{li2023blip2}), and our local tokens. Without decoder adaptation, the Multi-Token bridge is the best
of these five, and the bridges that compress the patches are 3.7--5.6 F1 lower despite
having up to $9.4\times$ more parameters.  Taking local tokens from the pre-trained projector instead works better: at the same $g=8$,
adding 36 or 144 of them improves F1 by 3.6 and 5.4 points (Table~\ref{tab:budget}). 

\paragraph{Local tokens and decoder adaptation are complementary.}
A decoder LoRA improves every global-only bridge and brings them within 0.8 F1 of one
another (52.69--53.47). Even after this adaptation, all of them remain below ViBridge-VQA with a frozen
decoder. Decoder adaptation is therefore not needed to surpass them, but it is still
useful: in the same one-epoch joint training, it adds 3.3 F1 to ViBridge-VQA, more than the
2.7 F1 it adds to the Multi-Token bridge. Distilling the projector into the global-only bridge instead lowers F1 by 0.3--0.4 points.

\paragraph{Number of global tokens.}
We varied the Multi-Token bridge from $g=4$ to $g=20$ tokens (three seeds each). Validation F1
rose from \prov{48.5} at $g=4$ to \prov{49.6} at $g=8$ and \prov{50.7} at $g=14$, and stayed
between \prov{50.4} and \prov{50.6} for $g=16$--$20$.

\subsection{Analysis by Reasoning Type and Error Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\paragraph{Reasoning types.}
Fig.~\ref{fig:results}b breaks the results down by reasoning category.
At the same $g=8$, adding 36 local tokens helps most where the answer depends on what is
visible in the image: recognition (+10.3 F1, Fig.~\ref{fig:results}b), spatial (+5.0) and
action (+3.7) questions. On these categories, this frozen-decoder model is also better than the
global-only model with a decoder LoRA. Conversely, decoder adaptation is more useful for
causal and relational questions, whose reference answers are the longest (5.0 and 5.6 words
on average), and for yes/no questions. Adding the LoRA to ViBridge-VQA combines both
strengths: the resulting model is the best of the four models in Fig.~\ref{fig:results}b in seven of the
eight categories, slightly above the global-only model with LoRA on causal (48.9 versus 48.3 F1) and relational
(60.5 versus 60.4) questions and well above it on recognition (58.7 versus 49.6); the only
exception is yes/no questions. 

\paragraph{Error analysis.}
On the validation predictions (all seeds), our answers are as long as the references (4.3 words on average). Most predictions (77--81\%) share some, but not all, words with the closest reference. Compared with the global-only model, ViBridge-VQA increases the share of predictions with F1 = 1 from 9.7\% to 13.3\% and reduces
the share with no word in common with any reference from 9.6\% to 8.0\% (6.8\% with LoRA).
Complete failures are most frequent on context, recognition, spatial and action questions
(12--18\%).

Fig.~\ref{fig:qual} illustrates how the two components work together. In every example,
ViBridge-VQA already identifies the relevant content of the image (the driver's phone,
the helmets, the football match, the bird feeder), but its answer remains vague.
Adding the decoder LoRA turns it into the precise answer, for instance that the driver is using
\emph{the phone} and that the helmets are worn \emph{to protect their heads}, in
line with the gains of decoder adaptation on causal and yes/no questions
(Section~\ref{sec:analysis}).
