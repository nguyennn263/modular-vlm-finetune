\section{Proposed Method}
\label{sec:method}

We formulate VQA as conditional answer generation. Given an image $I$ and a Vietnamese question
$q$, the model generates a short free-form answer $y=(y_1,\dots,y_T)$. ViBridge-VQA builds on
Vintern-1B~\cite{doan2024vintern1b} and keeps all of its pre-trained weights frozen. Instead of
adapting the model, it changes the visual input of its decoder.

\subsection{Overall Architecture}
\label{sec:encoding}

As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}, ViBridge-VQA keeps the three components of Vintern-1B: the
InternViT-300M vision encoder (24 layers, width 1024), its two-layer MLP projector $P$, and the
Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct decoder (24 layers, width 896). Each image is resized to one
$336\times336$ view, i.e., $24\times24$ patches of $14\times14$ pixels. InternViT, pre-trained at $448\times448$, bicubically interpolates its position embeddings to
this resolution, which roughly halves the encoder cost (191 instead of 362 GMACs per view). The encoder returns one
vector per patch and a global \texttt{[CLS]} vector $v\in\mathbb{R}^{1024}$. Vintern-1B uses only the patch vectors: it merges every $2\times2$ block of neighbouring patches into one 4096-dimensional vector (pixel shuffle~\cite{chen2024internvl15}). The projector $P$, pre-trained to align the two
backbones, maps each merged vector into the decoder input space. At our resolution, this yields a
$12\times12$ grid of 144 region tokens. ViBridge-VQA uses both outputs of the encoder.

\subsection{Global Tokens}
\label{sec:bridge}

The \texttt{[CLS]} vector summarises the whole scene, but Vintern-1B never passes it to the
decoder. A small trainable bridge $f_\phi$ expands it into $g$ tokens with two linear heads:
\begin{equation}
  f_\phi(v) = \big[\,W_a v + b_a;\ \mathrm{reshape}_{(g-1)\times 896}(W_e v + b_e)\,\big]
  \in \mathbb{R}^{g\times 896},
  \label{eq:bridge}
\end{equation}
where $W_a\in\mathbb{R}^{896\times1024}$ and $W_e\in\mathbb{R}^{(g-1)896\times1024}$. The first
head produces an \emph{anchor} token, and the second produces $g-1$ complementary tokens that present
the global content from different learned directions. The bridge has $1025\cdot896\cdot g$
parameters, i.e., 12{,}857{,}600 for $g=14$, and its $g$ tokens are placed before the prompt. To choose $g$, we varied the bridge alone from 4 to 20 tokens. F1 stops increasing at $g=14$
(Section~\ref{sec:budget-results}), which we use in ViBridge-VQA. We keep $g=8$ for our
Global-only model, as it matches the eight learned queries of the Tile-Attention and light
Q-Former bridges it is compared with (Section~\ref{sec:rq-source}). The local tokens are combined
with both values, and the final pair $(g,k)$ is selected with the criterion of
Section~\ref{sec:budget}.

\subsection{Local Tokens}
\label{sec:local}

To give the decoder region-level detail, ViBridge-VQA also passes local tokens taken directly from the 144 region tokens of the frozen projector $P$. To control their number, we
average-pool the $12\times12$ grid to an $s\times s$ grid, so that $k=s^2$ with
$s\in\{1,3,6,12\}$. Thus $k=144$ keeps every region token, and $k=36$ averages each $2\times2$
block. The pooled tokens replace the embeddings of $k$ image-context placeholders inside
Vintern-1B's own image slot, \texttt{<img>}\,$\cdots$\,\texttt{</img>}, in the prompt. Because they come from the pre-trained projector rather than from a newly learned projection,
the local tokens stay in the representation that the decoder was trained to read. Producing them
requires no parameters.

\subsection{Training Objective}
\label{sec:training}

Only the bridge parameters $\phi$ are trained. The vision encoder, the projector and the decoder
remain frozen, and no adapter is added to the decoder. We minimise the cross-entropy of the
answer tokens:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = -\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p\big(y_t \mid y_{<t},\, f_\phi(v),\, \mathrm{pool}_s(P(x)),\, q\big),
  \label{eq:ce}
\end{equation}
where $x$ denotes the merged patch vectors. ViBridge-VQA can also be combined with decoder
adaptation. In that variant, a rank-16 LoRA~\cite{hu2022lora} on the query, key, value and output
projections of every decoder layer is trained jointly with the bridge and adds 2{,}162{,}688 parameters. Throughout the paper, percentages of trained parameters are relative to all parameters of the model, which comprise the frozen components of Vintern-1B (about 0.94B, including the
vision encoder, projector, embeddings and output layer) plus the trained modules. With $g=14$, the
bridge alone trains 12.86M parameters (1.35\%), and the bridge with LoRA 15.02M (1.57\%).

\subsection{Choosing the Token Budget}
\label{sec:budget}

Since the cost of the decoder grows with the number of visual tokens $g+k$, we treat the pair
$(g,k)$ as a budget to be chosen. In our experiments, F1 increases with every added token among
the configurations with local tokens. All of them thus lie on the accuracy--token Pareto front,
and the front alone does not single one out. We therefore select its
\emph{knee}~\cite{satopaa2011kneedle}, the point after which additional tokens bring diminishing
returns. Here the accuracy $a$ is the validation F1 and the cost $c$ is the number of visual
tokens. We first normalise $c$ and $a$ to $[0,1]$ over the front. The knee is the
configuration that maximises $\hat a - \hat c$, i.e., the one furthest above the line joining the
cheapest and the most accurate configuration. The knee is computed on the validation split only, and we check the choice with the multi-criteria method TOPSIS~\cite{hwang1981topsis}, also on
validation. Test results are computed for every configuration only to be reported, and no choice
is based on them.
