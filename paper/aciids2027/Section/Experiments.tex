\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{Dataset.}
We evaluate on AutoViVQA~\cite{autovivqa2026}, a Vietnamese VQA benchmark built from
MS COCO images~\cite{lin2015coco} with an LLM-driven generation pipeline and
ensemble-based quality control. Each question has five short free-form answers
(1--10 words) and one of nine question types. The public release is a random
80/20 train/validation split (29{,}661 / 7{,}416 questions), in which questions about
the same image can fall into different splits. We remove 273 duplicated
(image, question) pairs and 97 questions with irregular type labels (77 of them of
the rare \emph{comparison} type), keeping eight reasoning categories, and
re-partition the remaining 36{,}707 questions by image into a grouped 70/15/15
split (seed 42), so that no image appears in more than one split
(Table~\ref{tab:data}). Compared with a random split, the grouped split lowers F1
by at most 0.5 points; we use it for every number reported here.

\paragraph{Evaluation metrics.}
Following AutoViVQA and ViMoE-VQA, we report answer-matching metrics (exact-match
Accuracy, token Precision, Recall and F1) and generation metrics
(BLEU~\cite{papineni2002bleu}, ROUGE-L~\cite{lin2004rouge},
METEOR~\cite{banerjee2005meteor}, CIDEr~\cite{vedantam2015cider}), all scaled by
100. Token-level scores are computed against each of the five references and the
best match is kept. Our F1 averages per-sample F1 scores and is therefore never
larger than the harmonic mean $2PR/(P+R)$ of averaged precision and recall, the
convention that matches most published baseline values; under that convention our
best configuration would score 55.76 instead of 54.52, so our F1 is conservative.

\paragraph{Implementation details.}
InternViT-300M and Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct are initialised from
Vintern-1B-v3.5~\cite{doan2024vintern1b} and kept frozen. Bridge-only models are
trained for 2 epochs, after which validation cross-entropy and CIDEr no longer
improve; ViBridge-VQA is trained for 1 or 3 epochs. We use AdamW with a learning
rate of $2\times10^{-4}$ and an effective batch size of 8, train on the first of the
five reference answers, and decode greedily. Every configuration is trained with
3 seeds (42, 123, 3407), and the bridge-only Multi-Token model additionally with
seed 2026. All runs fit on a single Tesla P100 (16\,GB). The test split
is used only for final reporting.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
We compare against (1) multimodal models fine-tuned on AutoViVQA (ViT5\_ViT,
BARTPhoBEiT~\cite{tran2023bartphobeitpretrainedsequencetosequenceimage} and
Vintern-1B~\cite{doan2024vintern1b}), (2) models used zero-shot (Vintern-1B,
LLaMA~3.2, Gemini~2.0 and 2.5 Flash, GPT-5), and (3) ViMoE-VQA~\cite{vimoevqa}.
Results of groups (1) and (2) are taken from the AutoViVQA benchmark and those of
ViMoE-VQA from its paper; both were obtained on an 8:1:1 split of the original
release. The fine-tuned Vintern-1B follows the official recipe of Vintern: the
vision encoder and the projector are frozen, a rank-16 LoRA is applied to the
language model, and each image is encoded as up to six $448\times448$ tiles plus a
thumbnail, for one epoch.

\paragraph{Improvement over Vintern-1B.}
Table~\ref{tab:main} shows that ViBridge-VQA substantially improves the model it is
built on. Starting from the same frozen Vintern-1B, it raises F1 from 17.55
(zero-shot) to 54.52 and CIDEr from 8.54 to 108.43. It also outperforms the
officially fine-tuned Vintern-1B on seven of the eight metrics: precision ($+2.9$),
recall ($+1.0$), F1 ($+0.8$), BLEU ($+14.9$), ROUGE-L ($+0.8$), METEOR ($+9.8$) and
CIDEr ($+35.6$); only exact-match accuracy is lower ($-1.6$). It does so while
encoding a single view and passing eight visual tokens to the decoder, instead of
up to seven views and 1{,}792 tokens. Even the bridge-only model (0.78\% of the
parameters) already exceeds the fine-tuned Vintern-1B on BLEU, METEOR and CIDEr.

\paragraph{Comparison with other systems.}
ViBridge-VQA achieves the best BLEU, ROUGE-L, METEOR and CIDEr among all compared
systems and exceeds ViMoE-VQA in exact-match accuracy (11.40 versus 9.55).
ViMoE-VQA, a task-specific model whose fusion module and decoder are trained on
AutoViVQA, keeps the highest precision and F1. Compared with zero-shot large
language models, ViBridge-VQA is better on every metric except recall: zero-shot
models produce long answers that cover many reference words at the cost of
precision, whereas ViBridge-VQA produces concise answers with a balanced
precision--recall trade-off.

\paragraph{Stability.}
All configurations are stable across random seeds (Table~\ref{tab:stability}): the
standard deviation is at most 0.22 for F1 and 1.10 for CIDEr, and test F1 lies only
0.26--0.37 points below validation F1, so no configuration is over-fitted to the
validation split. One epoch of joint training already provides about two thirds
of the 3-epoch gain. A paired bootstrap over the 5{,}463 validation questions
(2{,}000 resamples, seed 42) gives 95\% confidence intervals of $[2.20, 3.35]$ and
$[3.55, 4.79]$ F1 for the gain of the 1-epoch and 3-epoch models over the
bridge-only model.

ViBridge-VQA trains 9.51M parameters and encodes one view per image.
Table~\ref{tab:cost} shows the cost of the vision encoder for different numbers of
tiles: six tiles, as used by the fine-tuned Vintern-1B (plus a thumbnail), multiply
the encoder FLOPs by 6.0 and its latency by 4.0 compared with a single view. For the
Multi-Token bridge this extra cost brings no benefit: reading the \texttt{[CLS]}
vectors of six tiles gives 49.97 F1, compared with 50.87 for one view (seed 42).
Training is equally light: one epoch of ViBridge-VQA takes about 2.6 hours on a
single 16\,GB GPU.

\subsection{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:ablation}

All ablations are reported on the validation split.

\paragraph{Decoder adaptation across bridges.}
Table~\ref{tab:bridges} and Fig.~\ref{fig:results}a compare five bridges: a
\emph{Residual} bridge producing one token, the \emph{Multi-Token} bridge, a
\emph{Tile-Attention} bridge in which 8 learned queries attend over the patch
tokens, and a \emph{Light} and a \emph{Full Q-Former}~\cite{li2023blip2} with 8 and 16
queries. Without LoRA, the bridges span 45.14--50.74 F1, and the Multi-Token bridge
is the best and has the lowest validation cross-entropy; the Light and Full
Q-Formers, with $3.8\times$ and $9.4\times$ more parameters, are 3.7 and 3.9 F1
lower, so a larger bridge does not help a frozen decoder. Adding the decoder LoRA
improves \emph{every} bridge, although the 1-epoch joint run sees the data half as
often as the 2-epoch bridge-only run, and the gain is largest for the weakest
bridges ($+7.7$ for Tile-Attention, $+2.7$ for Multi-Token). After adaptation, the
bridges lie within 0.8 F1 (52.69--53.47) and 2.4 CIDEr points, compared with 5.6 F1
and 14.9 CIDEr points before: once the decoder can adapt, the choice of bridge
matters little, and decoder adaptation is the key ingredient of ViBridge-VQA.

\paragraph{Training signal and alignment.}
Table~\ref{tab:levers} tests whether the bridge-only model can be improved without
adapting the decoder. Sampling one of the five reference answers at random in each
epoch, instead of always the first, lowers F1 by 2.07. Distilling the bridge
towards the pre-aligned projector of Vintern-1B does not help either, at the
feature level ($-0.39$) or at the logit level with a small weight ($-0.28$,
$\lambda=0.1$), and degrades the model when the distillation term dominates
($-9.05$, $\lambda=1$). Decoder adaptation is the only intervention with a clear
gain ($+2.73$ and $+4.04$ F1).

\paragraph{Bridge design.}
Table~\ref{tab:design} examines two further design choices. Increasing the number
of output tokens $k$ raises F1 up to $k=14$, after which it plateaus; $k=8$, used
throughout the paper, lies within 1.1 F1 of the best value with fewer decoder
tokens, while $k=4$ and $k=6$ are clearly worse. Replacing the global vector with
bridges that pool the patch tokens confirms the design: fixed mean and max pooling
lose 10.8 and 13.9 F1, learned attention loses 5.1 F1, and a Honeybee-style
convolutional abstractor~\cite{cha2024honeybee} (convolution, adaptive average
pooling, convolution) comes within 0.7 F1 but needs $2.7\times$ the parameters. The
Multi-Token bridge thus offers the best trade-off between accuracy and size.

\subsection{Gains by Reasoning Type}

Fig.~\ref{fig:results}b breaks down the effect of decoder adaptation by the
reasoning categories of AutoViVQA. Joint training improves every category, and the
gain grows with the complexity of the expected answer: it is largest for causal
($+7.4$) and relational ($+4.8$) questions, whose reference answers are the longest
(5.0 and 5.6 words on average), and remains clear for yes/no ($+5.0$), spatial
($+4.4$) and recognition ($+4.3$) questions. Reasoning-intensive questions, the
focus of AutoViVQA, therefore benefit most from ViBridge-VQA.

To assess generalisation beyond AutoViVQA, we compare ViBridge-VQA (3 epochs) with
the zero-shot Vintern-1B (6 tiles) on two general-domain Vietnamese VQA datasets
that neither model was trained on, ViVQA~\cite{tran2021vivqa} and
ViVQA-X~\cite{vivqax2025} (Table~\ref{tab:ood}). For ViVQA we use the 505--519 sampled
questions whose COCO images could be retrieved, with the same images for both
models. Although it is adapted on AutoViVQA only and encodes a single view,
ViBridge-VQA is clearly better on ViVQA, where it improves six of the eight metrics
(F1 $+7.6$, ROUGE-L $+15.7$, CIDEr $+6.3$), and remains competitive on ViVQA-X (F1
$+1.4$, ROUGE-L $+3.0$, CIDEr $-8.0$), showing that the adaptation transfers beyond
its training benchmark. As on AutoViVQA, the zero-shot model reaches a high recall
with long answers but a low precision, whereas ViBridge-VQA answers concisely.

\subsection{Qualitative Analysis}

Fig.~\ref{fig:qual} shows representative predictions. In all four examples the
answers are grounded in the image but worded differently from the references:
``hugging the teddy bear'' instead of ``hugging the bear'' (Example~1), ``to avoid
the sun'' instead of ``to shade from the sun'' (Example~2), ``preparing to fly''
instead of ``preparing to take off'' (Example~3), and ``because they are very
happy'' instead of ``they are having fun'' (Example~4). The models generate their
own natural Vietnamese answers rather than reproducing reference phrasings; since
such paraphrases are only partly rewarded by token-level metrics, F1 and
exact-match accuracy tend to underestimate answer quality on open-ended questions.
