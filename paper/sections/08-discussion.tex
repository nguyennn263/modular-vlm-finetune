\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Four vision/training axes, one language axis: localizing the ceiling}

Sec.~\ref{sec:abl-bridge}--\ref{sec:abl-rq6} push on four vision- and
training-side levers that could, in principle, close the remaining F1 gap to
ViMoE-VQA on top of the frozen-backbone, 1-tile bridge --- bridge capacity,
visual-compute allocation, training target and representation alignment --- plus
one language-side lever, decoder capacity. The four vision/training axes are
negative and only the decoder axis is positive --- and even there, only when the
LoRA is placed on the decoder's \emph{attention} projections. The shape of that
split is itself the finding:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A bigger bridge does not help} (Sec.~\ref{sec:abl-bridge}). The
  $10\times$-larger Full Q-Former is \emph{worse} than the multi-token bridge on
  F1 ($-2.2$) and CIDEr-D, and the multi-token bridge has the lowest validation
  cross-entropy of all five. The bridge is not the constraint.
\item \textbf{Reasoning type does not predict visual-compute demand}
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:abl-routing}). No category benefits significantly from more
  tiles, and no learned policy --- reasoning-type-informed or not --- beats a
  fixed \bridge{multi\_token$|$t1} on held-out test, on either the original or the
  tile-augmented checkpoints.
\item \textbf{Multi-reference training and projector-alignment KD do not lift F1}
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:abl-rq5}). Resampling references each epoch (\dF{}~$-1.5$) and
  distilling toward Vintern's own pre-aligned projector (\dF{}~$-0.03$, an
  \emph{absolute} null) both fail: the bridge is already close to CE-optimal.
\item \textbf{Decoder-LoRA is the one lever that moves F1, and only through
  attention} (Sec.~\ref{sec:abl-rq6}). Adapting $0.23\%$ of the decoder lifts F1
  $+3.6$ on \multitoken{} and $+5.9$--$7.8$ on the four other bridges (larger for
  weaker bridges), collapsing the five into a $0.6$-point F1 band. Moving the same
  budget to the feed-forward layers diverges training (val loss $2$--$4$ vs.\
  $1.37$) --- possibly a hyperparameter artefact ($\alpha=32$ is aggressive for
  the wider MLP dimension), so we scope the claim to the recipe's settings.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Reading.}
It is not that we tried one clever trick and it happened to work; it is that
\emph{only} the trick which adds attention capacity to the decoder worked,
regardless of which bridge. For this VLM class --- frozen ViT, frozen small
(0.5B) decoder, a few pooled vision tokens --- the frozen decoder's attention is
the ceiling on token-level phrasing match, not the vision pipeline. We report
LoRA as a reference point quantifying that ceiling, not a replacement for the
paper's main spine
(Sec.~\ref{sec:results-main}--\ref{sec:results-corpus}) --- the frozen,
$0.78\%$-param bridge remains the primary contribution, and this finding explains
why that architecture class tops out where it does.

\subsection{Relation to ViMoE-VQA's ``reasoning-aware'' claim}

ViMoE-VQA attributes part of its MoE gain to ``approximate reasoning-aware expert
selection''. Our result does not contradict ViMoE's accuracy numbers, but it does
suggest the mechanism is mis-attributed: on the same benchmark, reasoning type
carries no signal about the \emph{optimal} visual-compute action, and ViMoE's own
leave-one-out ablation shows its experts are not strongly specialised. A more
parsimonious account of the MoE gain is added capacity / ensembling, not
reasoning-type routing. Testing this on ViMoE directly (does expert activation
correlate with question type?) is the obvious follow-up and requires only its
router logs.

\subsection{What is robust}

The \textbf{grouped split} closes an image-level leakage path; our bridge numbers
are within $\le 0.5$ F1 / $\le 3$ CIDEr-D of the random-split table (for the four
stable bridges --- one early Residual run was a training-instability outlier, val
CE $2.35$, fixed by the 3-seed re-run). The \textbf{multi-token bridge} result
holds across 4 seeds and on held-out test (F1 $49.2$ vs.\ val $49.6$). The
\textbf{null on adaptive routing} is cross-validated on two independently-trained
checkpoint generations. The \textbf{decoder-LoRA result} is the most-replicated
finding: $+3.6$ F1 on \multitoken{} (3/3 seeds) and $+5.9$--$7.8$ on four other
bridges, confirmed at corpus level and on test, with seed, bridge, metric and
attention-vs-MLP checks all agreeing.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{3-seed, not 5}; routing/oracle runs are seed 42. A 5-seed protocol
  is the camera-ready target.
\item \textbf{Frozen small decoder.} The negative vision/training results may not
  generalise to a trainable or larger decoder that \emph{can} use extra visual
  tokens --- Sec.~\ref{sec:abl-rq6}'s LoRA result is direct evidence for exactly
  that. We claim the negatives for the frozen-backbone / small-bridge regime only.
\item \textbf{The FFN-LoRA divergence is at the recipe's $\alpha=32$ and learning
  rate}; a gentler schedule might make it viable. Treat the direction and
  bridge-agnosticism of the LoRA effect as solid, the exact magnitude as
  provisional.
\item \textbf{$\ntiles \in \{1,3,6\}$ and three oracle bridges}; a finer grid or a
  non-pooling bridge might expose structure this study cannot see. Per-sample
  CIDEr is also noisy for 4-word answers.
\item \textbf{Human validation is reduced} to a single-rater self-check
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:human}); a 2-annotator, image-access study is the open item.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Ethical and reproducibility notes}

All models are frozen public checkpoints; only small bridges/heads are trained.
The split script, action space, and oracle tables are released. No human-subjects
data beyond the public AutoViVQA benchmark.
