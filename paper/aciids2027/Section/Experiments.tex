\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{Dataset.}
We evaluate on AutoViVQA~\cite{autovivqa2026}, a Vietnamese VQA benchmark built from
MS COCO images~\cite{lin2015coco} with an LLM-driven generation pipeline and
ensemble-based quality control. Each question has five short free-form answers
(1--10 words) and one of nine question types. The public release is a random
80/20 train/validation split (29{,}661 / 7{,}416 questions), in which questions about
the same image can fall into different splits. We remove 273 duplicated
(image, question) pairs and 97 questions with irregular type labels (77 of them of
the rare \emph{comparison} type), keeping eight reasoning categories, and
re-partition the remaining 36{,}707 questions by image into a grouped 70/15/15
split (seed 42), so that no image appears in more than one split
(Table~\ref{tab:data}). Compared with a random split, the grouped split lowers F1
by at most 0.5 points; we use it for every number reported here.

\paragraph{Evaluation metrics.}
Following AutoViVQA and ViMoE-VQA, we report answer-matching metrics (exact-match
Accuracy, token Precision, Recall and F1) and generation metrics
(BLEU~\cite{papineni2002bleu}, ROUGE-L~\cite{lin2004rouge},
METEOR~\cite{banerjee2005meteor}, CIDEr~\cite{vedantam2015cider}), all scaled by
100. Token-level scores are computed against each of the five references and the
best match is kept. Our F1 averages per-sample F1 scores and is therefore never
larger than the harmonic mean $2PR/(P+R)$ of averaged precision and recall, the
convention that matches most published baseline values; under that convention
ViBridge-VQA would score 55.95 instead of 54.56, so our F1 is conservative.

\paragraph{Implementation details.}
The vision encoder, the projector and the decoder are initialised from
Vintern-1B-v3.5~\cite{doan2024vintern1b} and kept frozen. Models without a decoder
LoRA, including ViBridge-VQA, are trained for 2 epochs, after which validation
cross-entropy and CIDEr no longer improve; the decoder-LoRA variants are trained for
1 or 3 epochs. We use AdamW with a learning rate of $2\times10^{-4}$ and an effective
batch size of 8, train on the first of the five reference answers, and decode
greedily. Every configuration is trained with 3 seeds (42, 123, 3407), and the
global-only Multi-Token model additionally with seed 2026. All runs fit on a single
16\,GB GPU (Tesla P100 or T4). The token budget is chosen on the validation split; the
test split is used only for reporting.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
We compare against (1) multimodal models fine-tuned on AutoViVQA (ViT5\_ViT,
BARTPhoBEiT~\cite{tran2023bartphobeitpretrainedsequencetosequenceimage} and
Vintern-1B~\cite{doan2024vintern1b}), (2) models used zero-shot (Vintern-1B,
LLaMA~3.2, Gemini~2.0 and 2.5 Flash, GPT-5), and (3) ViMoE-VQA~\cite{vimoevqa}.
Results of groups (1) and (2) are taken from the AutoViVQA benchmark and those of
ViMoE-VQA from its paper; both were obtained on an 8:1:1 split of the original
release. The fine-tuned Vintern-1B follows the official recipe of Vintern: the
vision encoder and the projector are frozen, a rank-16 LoRA is applied to the
language model, and each image is encoded as up to six $448\times448$ tiles plus a
thumbnail, for one epoch.

\paragraph{Improvement over Vintern-1B.}
Table~\ref{tab:main} shows that ViBridge-VQA substantially improves the model it is
built on without updating any of its weights. With the selected budget of 14 global
and 36 local tokens it raises F1 from 17.55 (zero-shot) to 54.56 and CIDEr from 8.54 to
110.58. It outperforms the officially fine-tuned Vintern-1B on seven of the eight
metrics, precision ($+2.9$), recall ($+1.4$), F1 ($+0.8$), BLEU ($+12.4$), ROUGE-L
($+0.7$), METEOR ($+10.3$) and CIDEr ($+37.7$), and is lower only in exact-match
accuracy ($-1.3$), while passing 50 visual tokens to the decoder instead of up to
1{,}792 and encoding one view instead of up to seven. With all 144 local tokens it
outperforms the fine-tuned Vintern-1B on all eight metrics, including accuracy
($+0.2$), F1 ($+2.3$), METEOR ($+12.1$) and CIDEr ($+43.9$), still with 11 times fewer
visual tokens.

\paragraph{Comparison with other systems.}
ViBridge-VQA achieves the best accuracy, ROUGE-L, METEOR and CIDEr among all compared
systems; the best BLEU belongs to the global-only model with a decoder LoRA.
ViMoE-VQA, a task-specific model whose fusion module and decoder are trained on
AutoViVQA, keeps the highest precision and F1. Compared with zero-shot large language
models, ViBridge-VQA is better on every metric except recall: zero-shot models produce
long answers that cover many reference words at the cost of precision, whereas
ViBridge-VQA produces concise answers with a balanced precision--recall trade-off.
Even with a frozen decoder, ViBridge-VQA matches the global-only model trained with a
3-epoch decoder LoRA (54.56 versus 54.52 F1) and surpasses it with $k=144$.

\paragraph{Stability.}
All configurations are stable across random seeds (Table~\ref{tab:stability}): the
standard deviation of ViBridge-VQA is at most 0.28 for F1 and 1.01 for CIDEr, and
test F1 lies 0.4--0.6 points below validation F1, so the budget chosen on the
validation split does not over-fit it.

Table~\ref{tab:budget} and Fig.~\ref{fig:results}a vary the number of global tokens
$g\in\{8,14\}$ and local tokens $k\in\{1,9,36,144\}$. \emph{Local tokens matter far
more than global ones}: at $g=14$, going from 1 to 144 local tokens adds 4.9 F1 and 19.1
CIDEr points on validation, whereas going from 8 to 14 global tokens adds 0.2--0.6 F1
at a fixed $k$. Accuracy does not saturate within the 144 region tokens available at
our resolution: each step of $k$ in Table~\ref{tab:budget} adds 1.3--2.0 F1. The gain per added
token, however, falls quickly, from 0.07 F1 per token between 23 and 50 tokens to 0.015
between 50 and 158, so the budget is a genuine trade-off.

Since every configuration lies on the Pareto front, we select the knee
(Section~\ref{sec:budget}). On the validation split it is $g=14$, $k=36$, and the same
configuration is the knee for each of the eight metrics on both validation and test,
both when cost is counted in visual tokens and when it is measured as inference
time; with a logarithmic token axis it is the knee in 11 of 16 cases. As a
multi-criteria check, TOPSIS with the gains over the global-only model in F1, CIDEr
and METEOR as benefits and the added tokens, inference time, trained parameters and
seed standard deviation as costs ranks this configuration first when accuracy and
cost are weighted equally; weighting cost more favours $k=9$ and weighting accuracy
more favours $k=144$, but $g=14$, $k=36$ is the only configuration among the top four
for every accuracy weight from 0.3 to 0.7. It is also the cheapest configuration that
reaches the global-only model with a 3-epoch decoder LoRA (54.96 versus 54.78
validation F1), and it obtains 72\% of the largest F1 gain (4.2 of 5.9 points) with
32\% of the tokens and 15\% less inference time than $k=144$. We use 14 global tokens
because they are better than 8 at every $k$ (by 0.5--0.6 F1 at $k=1$, 36 and 144, and
by 0.2 at $k=9$), in line with the earlier observation that the global-only bridge
plateaus at 14 tokens (Table~\ref{tab:design}a).

ViBridge-VQA trains 12.86M parameters, encodes one view per image and passes 50 visual
tokens to the decoder. Table~\ref{tab:cost} shows the cost of the vision encoder for
different numbers of tiles: six tiles, as used by the fine-tuned Vintern-1B (plus a

an evaluation session about 17\% slower (Table~\ref{tab:budget}).

\subsection{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:ablation}

All ablations are reported on the validation split.

\paragraph{Global-only bridges and decoder adaptation.}
Table~\ref{tab:bridges} compares five bridges that do not use the projector of
Vintern-1B: a \emph{Residual} bridge producing one token, the \emph{Multi-Token}
bridge over the \texttt{[CLS]} vector, a \emph{Tile-Attention} bridge in which 8 learned
queries attend over the patch tokens, and a \emph{Light} and a \emph{Full}
Q-Former~\cite{li2023blip2} with 8 and 16 queries. Without decoder adaptation they
span 45.14--50.74 F1, and the Multi-Token bridge is the best: the bridges that learn
to compress the patches, with up to $9.4\times$ more parameters, are 3.7--5.6 F1
lower, so learning a new alignment of the patch tokens from 25k questions does not
pay off. A decoder LoRA improves every bridge and brings them within 0.8 F1
(52.69--53.47). ViBridge-VQA takes a different route: it keeps the decoder frozen but
gives it local tokens that are already aligned with it, and reaches 54.96 F1 with
$k=36$ and 56.61 with $k=144$, above every bridge with a decoder LoRA. Decoder
adaptation therefore compensates for poor visual tokens rather than being required.
Two further interventions on the global-only bridge confirm that the projector's
information must be passed as tokens rather than distilled: training the bridge
towards the projector of Vintern-1B, at the feature level or on the decoder's output
distribution, lowers F1 by 0.39 and 0.28 (and by 9.05 when the distillation term
dominates), and sampling a random reference answer per epoch lowers it by 2.07.

\paragraph{Bridge design.}
Table~\ref{tab:design} examines two design choices of the global-only bridge.
Increasing its number of output tokens raises F1 up to 14 tokens, after which it
plateaus, which motivates $g=14$. Replacing the global vector with bridges that pool
the raw patch tokens through a newly learned projection loses accuracy: fixed mean and
max pooling lose 10.8 and 13.9 F1, learned attention loses 5.1 F1, and a
Honeybee-style convolutional abstractor~\cite{cha2024honeybee} comes within 0.7 F1 with
$2.7\times$ the parameters. Pooling the output of the pre-trained projector instead,
as ViBridge-VQA does, turns the same kind of operator into a gain: with the same 8
global tokens, adding the region tokens averaged over $2\times2$ blocks ($k=36$) raises
F1 by 3.6 points (Table~\ref{tab:budget}).

\subsection{Gains by Reasoning Type}

Fig.~\ref{fig:results}b breaks the results down by the reasoning categories of
AutoViVQA. The local tokens help most where the answer depends on what is visible in
the image: recognition ($+10.2$ F1 over the global-only model), action ($+5.4$) and
spatial ($+5.1$) questions, and ViBridge-VQA is better than the decoder-LoRA model on
all three as well as on counting and context. The decoder-LoRA model is better on
causal and relational questions, whose reference answers are the longest (5.0 and 5.6
words on average), and on the 66 yes/no questions; it also has the best BLEU and
cross-entropy (Table~\ref{tab:stability}). Local tokens thus improve what the model
perceives, while decoder adaptation improves how it phrases reasoning answers, which
suggests that the two are complementary.

\subsection{Qualitative Analysis}

Fig.~\ref{fig:qual} shows representative predictions. In the first three examples
only ViBridge-VQA answers correctly, and each answer depends on a local detail: the
brand printed on the laptop (Example~1), the buildings and boats that identify Venice
(Example~2), and the water sprayed by the elephant (Example~3). The global-only
models, with or without a decoder LoRA, give plausible but wrong answers such as
``Apple'', ``Hanoi'' or ``swimming''. Example~4 shows a failure: asked why a bus has
stopped, ViBridge-VQA describes the scene (``going along the road''), whereas both
global-only models infer the purpose (``to pick up passengers''), consistent with the
advantage of decoder adaptation on causal questions.
