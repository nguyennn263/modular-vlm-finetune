\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

\subsection{Dataset and Evaluation Metrics}

\paragraph{Dataset.}
We evaluate on AutoViVQA~\cite{autovivqa2026}, a Vietnamese VQA benchmark built from
MS COCO images~\cite{lin2015coco} with an LLM-driven generation pipeline and
ensemble-based quality control. Each question has five short free-form answers
(1--10 words) and is labelled with one of nine question types. The public
release is a random 80/20 train/validation split (29{,}661 / 7{,}416 questions),
in which questions about the same image can fall into different splits. We
remove 273 duplicated (image, question) pairs and 97 questions with irregular
type labels, 77 of which belong to the rare \emph{comparison} type, and keep eight
reasoning categories. We then re-partition the remaining
36{,}707 questions by image into a grouped 70/15/15 split (seed 42), so that no
image appears in more than one split (Table~\ref{tab:data}). The category
distribution is kept approximately equal across splits. On our bridges, the
grouped split lowers F1 by at most 0.5 points compared with a random split, so
image leakage has a small effect, but we use the grouped split for every number
reported here.

\paragraph{Evaluation metrics.}
Following AutoViVQA and ViMoE-VQA, we report answer-matching metrics (exact-match
Accuracy, token Precision, Recall and F1) and generation metrics
(BLEU~\cite{papineni2002bleu}, ROUGE-L~\cite{lin2004rouge},
METEOR~\cite{banerjee2005meteor}, CIDEr~\cite{vedantam2015cider}), all scaled by
100. Token-level scores are computed against each of the five references and the
best match is kept. Our F1 is the mean of per-sample F1 scores, which is never
larger than the harmonic mean $2PR/(P+R)$ of the averaged precision and recall.
Most published baseline F1 values match the latter convention; under it, our best
configuration would score 55.76 instead of 54.52 on test, so our F1 values are
conservative.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{Model configuration.}
InternViT-300M and Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct are initialised from
Vintern-1B-v3.5~\cite{doan2024vintern1b} and kept frozen. The bridge-only models
are trained for 2 epochs, after which validation cross-entropy and CIDEr no longer
improve; the joint bridge + LoRA models are trained for 1 or 3 epochs, each from a
randomly initialised bridge. We use AdamW with a learning rate of
$2\times10^{-4}$ and an effective batch size of 8. Training uses the first of the
five reference answers and evaluation uses all five. Answers are generated with
greedy decoding. A bridge run takes about 5 hours on a single Tesla P100 (16\,GB).
Each configuration is trained with 3 seeds (42, 123, 3407); the Multi-Token bridge
additionally uses seed 2026. The test split is only used for final reporting.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
We compare against three groups: (1) multimodal models fine-tuned on AutoViVQA
(ViT5\_ViT, BARTPhoBEiT~\cite{tran2023bartphobeitpretrainedsequencetosequenceimage}
and Vintern-1B~\cite{doan2024vintern1b}); (2) models used zero-shot (Vintern-1B,
LLaMA~3.2, Gemini~2.0 and 2.5 Flash, GPT-5); and (3)
ViMoE-VQA~\cite{vimoevqa}. Results of groups (1) and (2) are taken from the
AutoViVQA benchmark~\cite{autovivqa2026} and the results of ViMoE-VQA from its
paper; both were obtained on an 8:1:1 split of the original release and are
therefore not strictly comparable with our grouped split. The fine-tuned
Vintern-1B follows the official fine-tuning recipe of
Vintern~\cite{doan2024vintern1b}: the vision encoder and the projector are
frozen, a rank-16 LoRA is applied to the language model, each image is encoded
as up to six $448\times448$ tiles plus a thumbnail, and training runs for one
epoch. It therefore trains a comparable fraction of parameters (about 0.2\% versus
1.01\% for ViBridge-VQA), but encodes up to seven views per image and passes 256
visual tokens per view to the decoder, whereas ViBridge-VQA encodes one view and
passes 8 tokens.

Table~\ref{tab:main} compares ViBridge-VQA with the baselines.

\paragraph{Improvement over Vintern-1B.}
ViBridge-VQA substantially improves the model it is built on. Starting from the
same frozen Vintern-1B, it raises F1 from 17.55 (zero-shot) to 54.52 and CIDEr from
8.54 to 108.43. More importantly, it also outperforms the officially fine-tuned
Vintern-1B on seven of the eight metrics: precision ($+2.9$), recall ($+1.0$), F1
($+0.8$), BLEU ($+14.9$), ROUGE-L ($+0.8$), METEOR ($+9.8$) and CIDEr ($+35.6$); only
exact-match accuracy is lower ($-1.6$). The fine-tuned Vintern-1B encodes each image
as up to six $448\times448$ tiles plus a thumbnail and passes 256 visual tokens per
tile to the decoder, whereas ViBridge-VQA encodes a single view and passes eight
tokens, i.e.\ at most one sixth of the encoder cost (Table~\ref{tab:cost}). Even the
bridge alone (0.78\% of the parameters) already exceeds the fine-tuned Vintern-1B
on BLEU, METEOR and CIDEr.

\paragraph{Comparison with other systems.}
ViBridge-VQA achieves the best BLEU, ROUGE-L, METEOR and CIDEr among all compared
systems, including ViMoE-VQA, which it also exceeds in exact-match accuracy
(11.40 versus 9.55). ViMoE-VQA, a task-specific model whose fusion module and
decoder are trained on AutoViVQA, keeps the highest precision and F1. Compared with zero-shot large
language models, ViBridge-VQA is better on every metric except recall: as observed
for AutoViVQA and ViMoE-VQA, zero-shot models produce long answers that cover many
reference words at the cost of precision, whereas ViBridge-VQA produces concise
answers with a balanced precision--recall trade-off. One epoch of joint training
already provides about two thirds of the 3-epoch F1 gain.

\paragraph{Statistical reliability.}
ViBridge-VQA is stable across random seeds (Table~\ref{tab:stability}): the
standard deviation of F1 is at most 0.22 and that of CIDEr at most 1.10. Test F1
is 0.26--0.37 points below validation F1 for all three configurations, which shows
that no configuration was over-fitted to the validation split. A paired bootstrap
over the 5{,}463 validation questions (2{,}000 resamples, seed 42) gives 95\%
confidence intervals of $[2.20, 3.35]$ and $[3.55, 4.79]$ for the F1 gain of the
1-epoch and 3-epoch LoRA models over the bridge-only model.

\paragraph{Computational cost.}
ViBridge-VQA trains 9.51M parameters and encodes one view per image.
Table~\ref{tab:cost} reports the cost of the vision encoder for different numbers
of tiles: moving from one view to six tiles, the setting we use for the zero-shot
Vintern-1B, multiplies the encoder FLOPs by 6.0 and its latency by 4.0. Since the bridge uses one global
vector, this cost is not needed: feeding six tiles to the single-view bridge and
reading the CLS vector of each tile gives F1 49.97, compared with 50.87 for one
view (seed 42).

\subsection{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:ablation}

All ablations are measured on the validation split and compared with the
bridge-only Multi-Token model (F1 50.74).

\paragraph{Bridge architecture and decoder adaptation.}
Table~\ref{tab:bridges} and Fig.~\ref{fig:results}a compare the bridges. Without
LoRA, they span 45.14--50.74 F1. The Multi-Token bridge is the best and has the
lowest validation cross-entropy, while the Light and Full Q-Formers, with $3.8\times$
and $9.4\times$ more parameters, are 3.7 and 3.9 F1 lower: a larger bridge does not
help a frozen decoder.
Training with decoder LoRA improves \emph{every} bridge, even though the joint
1-epoch run sees the data half as often as the 2-epoch bridge-only run, and the
improvement is largest for the weakest bridges ($+7.7$ for Tile-Attention, $+6.7$
for Residual, $+2.7$ for Multi-Token). After LoRA, the bridges lie within 0.8 F1
(52.69--53.47) and 2.4 CIDEr points, compared with 5.6 F1 and 14.9 CIDEr points
before. Once the decoder can adapt, the choice of bridge therefore matters little.

\paragraph{Training signal and representation alignment.}
Table~\ref{tab:levers} tests whether the bridge can be improved without touching
the decoder. Sampling one of the five reference answers at random in each epoch,
instead of always the first, lowers F1 by 2.07. Distilling the bridge towards the
pre-aligned projector of Vintern-1B does not help at the feature level ($-0.39$)
or at the logit level with a small weight ($-0.28$, $\lambda=0.1$), and degrades the
model when the distillation term dominates ($-9.05$, $\lambda=1$). The bridge is
therefore already as well aligned as the frozen decoder can exploit, and the only
intervention with a clear gain is decoder adaptation.

\paragraph{Bridge design: number of tokens and pooling.}
Two further design choices of the bridge are examined in
Tables~\ref{tab:ntokens} and~\ref{tab:pooling}. Varying the number of output tokens
$k$ of the Multi-Token bridge shows that F1 increases from $k=4$ to $k=14$ and then
plateaus; $k=8$, used throughout the paper, lies within 1.1 F1 of the best value
while passing fewer tokens to the decoder, and fewer tokens ($k=4,6$) are clearly
worse. Replacing the global vector by bridges that pool the patch tokens confirms
the design: fixed mean or max pooling loses 10.8 and 13.9 F1, learned attention
loses 5.1 F1, and a Honeybee-style convolutional abstractor~\cite{cha2024honeybee}
(convolution, adaptive average pooling, convolution) comes within 0.7 F1 but needs
$2.7\times$ the parameters. The Multi-Token bridge thus offers the best trade-off
between accuracy and size.

\subsection{Analysis by Reasoning Type}

Fig.~\ref{fig:results}b breaks down the effect of decoder adaptation by the
reasoning categories of AutoViVQA. Joint training improves almost every category,
and the gain grows with the complexity of the expected answer: it is largest for
causal ($+7.4$) and relational ($+4.8$) questions, whose reference answers are the
longest (5.0 and 5.6 words on average), and remains clear for yes/no ($+5.0$),
spatial ($+4.4$) and recognition ($+4.3$) questions. Reasoning-intensive questions,
the focus of AutoViVQA, thus benefit most from adapting the decoder.
